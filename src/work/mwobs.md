## What Is Mist?

<p class="meta">Monday, 10:45 p.m.</p>

What is mist (BR)? When most people think of mist, they envision light droplets of water falling on the trench-coated shoulders of their favorite detective; however, mist is actually classified as an obstruction to vision. In METAR code, mist is defined as “a visible aggregate of minute water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the atmosphere that reduces visibility to less than seven statute miles but greater than or equal to 5/8 statute mile” (FMH-1 Handbook). In this respect, mist is similar to fog, though fog reduces visibility to less than 5/8 statute mile. The METAR acronym for mist, BR, comes from the French word brume, a reminder that METAR acronyms are a mélange of French and English weather terms.

Qu’est-ce que la brume (BR) ? La plupart des gens pensent à une légère précipitation trempant les épaules de M. Maigret lorsqu’ils imaginent la brume ; et pourtant, la brume n’est pas une forme de précipitation mais plutôt un obstacle à la visibilité. Selon le code METAR, la brume est définie comme de microscopiques gouttelettes d’eau liquide ou de cristaux de glace en suspension près du sol qui donnent une visibilité entre sept milles et 5/8 mille. Dans ce sens, elle ressemble au brouillard, bien que celui-ci réduise la visibilité à moins de 5/8 mille. Le sigle METAR, BR, est évidemment un vestige du code METAR français.

_Steve Bailey, Observer_

## Blowing Snow vs. Low Drifting Snow

<p class="meta">Thursday, 7:00 p.m.</p>

<figure>
  <img src="/optim/article/img/mwo-commute.JPG" alt="A group preparing to descend Mount Washington in winter" width="1600" height="1200" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Winter commuting from the “Home of the World's Worst Weather”.</figcaption>
</figure>

What is the difference between blowing snow and low drifting snow? Both blowing snow (BLSN)\* and low drifting snow (DRSN)\* are lifted off the earth’s surface by the wind; however, blowing snow is lifted six feet or more off the ground, whereas low drifting snow is lifted less than six feet. In addition, blowing snow reduces horizontal visibility to less than seven statute miles.

Quelle est la différence entre la poudrerie élevée (la chasse-neige élevée) et la poudrerie basse (la chasse-neige basse) ? La poudrerie élevée (BLSN)\* et la poudrerie basse (DRSN)\* sont toutes les deux soulevées par le vent de la surface terrestre ; et pourtant, la poudrerie élevée est soulevée à des hauteurs de deux mètres ou plus, tandis que la poudrerie basse est soulevée à moins de deux mètres. En plus, la poudrerie élevée réduit la visibilité à moins de 11 km.

\*The acronym in parentheses is the actual METAR code for the phenomenon. / Le sigle entre parenthèses est le code METAR du phénomène météorologique.

_Steve Bailey, Observer_

## From the Secret METAR Archives

<p class="meta">Saturday, 4:00 p.m.</p>

In burrowing through the hidden METAR archives, located in a dank, candlelit chamber deep within the mountain itself, I stumbled upon a reference to an arcane METAR acronym that has rather lamentably been withheld from the world for some time. Not even METAR guru Eric Pinder has laid eyes upon this gem. Normally, my routine lucubrations produce no such treasures… but last night all that changed.

Although the archive room and its musty contents are supposed to remain a secret, I have decided to share my delicious find. On page 1,449 of a dust-covered tome entitled The Secrets of the Universe, there is a passage describing peculiar wind patterns found precisely at the confluence of the Allegheny, Monongahela, and Ohio Rivers. Similar to the Bermuda Triangle, this unique stretch of earth produces swirling currents of air said to have inspired Carnegie to build his libraries, museums, and castles.

Conditions conducive to these Winds of Inspiration are forecast for Sunday the 27th, and thus it seems an appropriate time to dust off the obscure acronym and bring it out of retirement. So, tomorrow around 11:59 a.m., look for a most unusual condition in our METAR report: GO PATS!

_Steve Bailey, Observer_
